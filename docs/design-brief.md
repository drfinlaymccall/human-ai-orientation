# Human AI Orientation — initial design brief

Date: 2026-10-13. Stage: discovery. This is a working brief requested during the interview, not a settled product contract or implementation-ready plan. Confirmed intentions and proposals are distinguished below.

## Purpose and success

Help people who are not AI specialists establish a productive, informed relationship with AI through useful tasks, better context, and capability discovery. The course should respect their time and let them judge whether a capability is worth using in their own lives.

**Design commitment:** a learner can complete this orientation independently and leave better equipped to identify a worthwhile personal or professional goal, explain their context, and begin purposeful collaboration with their own AI agent. Their personal context portfolio includes ideas for future work and can travel with them into Finlay's later human–agent curriculum-design programme.

An equally explicit objective is to demonstrate that Finlay McCall can bring a whole project together to create an effective digital learning experience. Project management and delivery lead the portfolio story; curriculum design connects needs, content, activities, support, and the learner journey. Evaluation supports quality and improvement, with a proportionate role in this self-access course.

The priority order is: end-to-end delivery of a useful learning experience; coherent curriculum and experience design; practical platform, content, and support operations; and evidence-led improvement. This builds on Finlay's University of Canberra project work without implying university sponsorship or endorsement of this independent project.

The project should become a working public course on a subdomain of Finlay's website, accompanied by a GitHub project and a readable design case study. Friends and family are the initial pilot audience. Finlay intends to write about the project and launch it on LinkedIn to invite enrolments; publication timing and copy remain to be developed.

## Place in the broader learning journey

The orientation is a self-contained human-learning experience within a broader proposed pathway:

| Stage | Purpose | Relationship to this build |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Needs discovery | Identify what someone wants to achieve and whether foundational AI orientation would help | A needs-analysis agent could recommend this course. This is a possible entry route, not a required integration or enrolment gate |
| Human orientation | Develop the person's understanding, practical interaction skills, goal awareness, and ability to describe relevant context | The current product and the entire scope of this implementation |
| Human–agent curriculum design | Turn a chosen goal into a learning programme for the person and agent: identify their respective needs, develop specialist content, and construct evaluations of the agent's performance | A separate future implementation whose consumer-facing form is not yet settled |

In orientation, learners practise recognising what they need to learn and what their agent needs to be told or shown. The later programme develops that distinction into a substantive curriculum. Here, “educating the agent” describes the intended later collaboration; it does not commit either product to model fine-tuning or a particular technical training method.

The bridge is a learner-owned context-and-goal document. Orientation must remain useful even if the later programme is unavailable or the learner chooses another next step. Do not require shared accounts, automatic data transfer, a built-in needs-analysis agent, or an embedded AI chat to deliver that bridge. Course prompts and learner-selected context can be copied into the learner's own AI tool, with brief reflections brought back to the course.

## Learners and positioning

The core audience can use a device, email, and everyday online services but does not specialise in AI. Do not label learners as deficient or assume low confidence. Some are confident but busy, uncertain about the payoff, or uninterested in tinkering. Discover experience, confidence, goals, accessibility needs, available time, device, and preferred way of getting started.

The core assumes no AI expertise. Optional challenges serve people already using AI professionally. Provide explanation, worked demonstration, and practice as complementary choices; do not require unguided experimentation to understand a task or assign fixed learning-style labels.

Finlay's examples suggest two problems worth testing: people may not recognise what a new capability could do for them, and they may not understand how personal or project context affects results. These are initial qualitative observations, not evidence of market demand or proven course outcomes.

Existing introductory courses are potential supporting resources. The proposed distinction is a personally relevant, supported orientation that helps learners establish a working practice and leave with useful resources of their own. Do not claim that no comparable course exists.

## Confirmed design intentions

- Bring your own AI: learners work in a tool they choose, then report back in the course.
- Use task-based learning and a communicative approach: learners explain intentions, negotiate meaning, clarify, correct, and evaluate with the AI.
- Give learners enough understanding of AI to make better decisions about its use.
- Make context and capability discovery central, including personal context, project context, and persistence across interactions.
- Keep the core accessible to non-specialists, with optional extensions for experienced users.
- Provide accounts, saved progress, course updates, reminders or refreshers, and short checks of understanding.
- Link and attribute external resources rather than reproduce them without permission.
- Prefer open-source, modifiable technology that can be hosted affordably.
- Offer self-guided learning with a way to ask Finlay for help. An agent drafts support responses for Finlay's approval before sending.
- Treat the learning-design portfolio as a first-class deliverable throughout the project.
- Lead the portfolio with project management and the delivery of an effective digital learning experience, with curriculum design as the connecting thread.
- Include labelled artificial-persona walkthroughs as a way to rehearse and evaluate the experience before and alongside human participation.
- Equip the human to identify useful goals and context-specific learning needs, and leave with a portable personal context portfolio that supports later collaboration.
- Keep the human–agent curriculum-design programme a separate implementation; this course prepares learners to enter it without depending on it.

## Learning approach

### Scholarly positioning

This is an introductory course in AI literacy and purposeful human–AI collaboration, developed through instructional and learning design. Its learners encounter early practices of needs analysis and evaluation; training them as instructional designers belongs beyond orientation.

The recommended framing is **a needs-led, task-based human AI orientation grounded in Finlay's curriculum and course-design research, with explicit support for metacognition and self-evaluation**. The thesis and Human Agent Curriculum Design materials provide the primary lineage; instructional-design models support development. Finlay's needs/content/evaluation/execution loop is a practical adaptation, distinct from the thesis's five-domain framework and from ADDIE.

Use the literature at distinct levels:

| Design level | Principal reference | Concrete implication here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary curriculum and course-design lineage | McCall (2025), section 2.4.2 and Appendix B; its discussion of Graves, Richards, needs analysis and curriculum coherence; current Human Agent Curriculum Design philosophy and lifecycle | Treat needs, rationale, content, enactment and evaluation as interacting decisions; preserve learner agency and revise from experience. Adapt the thesis's domain-specific principles to orientation |
| Knowledge and metacognition | Revised Bloom taxonomy (Anderson, Krathwohl and colleagues, 2001; Krathwohl, 2002); McCall's Appendix B | Align knowledge types and cognitive processes with small tasks; learners explain strategy choices, judge their own performance and revise guidance. The taxonomy is not a compulsory ladder or a teaching method |
| Developing the course | Branch's treatment of ADDIE (2009); Dick, Carey and Carey (2022) | Analyse learners and context, specify outcomes and evidence, develop the experience, pilot it, and revise; evaluation informs development throughout |
| Aligning curriculum | Biggs (1996), constructive alignment | A promised capability must appear in the task and in the evidence of learning; recognising terminology alone cannot demonstrate practical collaboration |
| Structuring activities | Merrill (2002), first principles of instruction | Begin with a meaningful task, connect prior experience, show an example, provide guided application, and help learners reuse the skill in their own setting |
| Developing human capacity | Zimmerman (2002); Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) | Support goal setting, monitoring, self-assessment, criteria and revision; self-access describes delivery, while self-regulation is a capability we deliberately support |
| Communicating through tasks | Ellis (2014), task-based language teaching | Adapt purposeful communication, clarification and task outcomes to human–AI work; this transfer from language education is a design hypothesis to test |
| Learning with technology | Laurillard (2012) | Combine explanation, inquiry and practice, and document reusable design decisions; an AI conversation alone is not evidence that a complete conversational learning framework has been implemented |
| Defining AI literacy | Long and Magerko (2020) | Include critical judgement and effective interaction with AI for non-specialists, with no programming prerequisite |

Sources, verified access, interpretation limits, and references are in the [scholarly design rationale](research/scholarly-design-rationale.md). These relationships are proposed design commitments grounded in literature, not evidence that the unbuilt course is already effective. Keep the theoretical vocabulary in the design documentation and optional case study; learner-facing activities use ordinary language.

### The four pillars in practice

Finlay's high-level design loop is **needs → content → evaluation → execution**, feeding evidence back into the next needs analysis. These are the underlying design pillars for orientation. Introduce them through ordinary tasks and brief reflection; learners do not need to learn the framework's terminology or master curriculum design to complete the course.

| Pillar | Human capacity to begin developing | Light-touch expression in orientation | Deeper work in the later programme |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Needs | Identify needs through self-assessment and self-evaluation | “What can I already do, what am I unsure about, and what would help?” Revisit after trying a task | Identify and distinguish human, agent, and context-specific learning needs |
| Content and knowledge | Identify and evaluate existing knowledge, and bring useful ideas together | Choose a relevant note, example, or source; consider why it is suitable and what might be missing; put the useful parts into a short explanation | Select, evaluate, organise, and synthesise a substantive body of specialist knowledge |
| Assessment and evaluation | Anticipate what needs checking and how to judge progress | Before trying, name one sign of a useful result; during and afterwards, check it and reconsider the next step | Develop assessments and evaluations of human learning and agent performance throughout the curriculum |
| Execution | Put the learning design into practice and respond to what happens | The human explains the task, supplies selected context, asks the agent to try, checks the result, and adjusts the guidance | Finlay's teaching agent is the execution layer in the broader design; its implementation remains separate |

The ordering is a design aid, not a rigid sequence. Evaluation is anticipated before execution and continues throughout: learners assess their starting point, judge source material, inspect results, and revisit needs. Synthesis begins as a small act of making sense of useful information, not a literature-review assignment.

Humans initially perform the teaching/execution role themselves. The orientation gives them enough guidance to enact that role with their own AI. Distinguish the learner's chosen AI, the course's human-reviewed support-drafting agent, and the later teaching agent: they have different roles, and the teaching agent is not an MVP dependency.

At course-design level, identify learner needs, select tasks and supporting explanations, define evidence of capability, run the learning experience, and revise from observations.

At activity level, the proposed translation is:

1. Name something the learner wants to do, what they can already do, and where they need help.
2. Offer a short explanation and an example; help them select, question, and summarise relevant context or existing knowledge.
3. Agree what would make the result useful and what needs checking before starting.
4. Guide their own AI through the task, checking, clarifying, and correcting as they go.
5. Judge the result and their own progress, report what changed, and revisit the next need or step.

Assess useful outcomes and judgement rather than prompt-writing jargon. A learner can complete an activity by finding that AI is unsuitable for that task and explaining why.

## Take-away commitment: personal context and future goals

Learners leave with a portable personal context portfolio, including ideas they would like to pursue with AI. The proposed beginner-friendly form is a small context kit that can grow with them:

- **Working with me:** relevant preferences, goals, constraints, and how the learner wants help.
- **This task or project:** purpose, necessary background, source material, boundaries, and what a good result would look like.
- **What I might do next:** possible personal or professional uses, one provisional goal worth exploring, why it matters, and a manageable first step.
- **What we still need:** what the human wants to understand or practise, and what background or examples the agent would need for the chosen goal.
- **What I can draw on:** useful existing knowledge or material, why it is relevant, and a short account of what it contributes or leaves uncertain.
- **Keep it useful:** where the learner keeps these notes, what they choose to share, and when to review or remove outdated information.
- **Reusable guidance:** a short personal instruction brief and a project instruction brief derived selectively from the portfolio. Introduce `CLAUDE.md` and `AGENTS.md` as concrete file conventions; learners can create the text themselves and understand where it would apply.

Learners should be able to keep this in an ordinary document or notes app. Copying the text is a sufficient portability mechanism for the MVP. Markdown, GitHub, API keys, MCP, and paid AI plans should not be prerequisites. Whether the course offers optional storage or export tools is still open; the recommended starting point is to store reflections and progress in the course while learners retain the context kit themselves. A [draft takeaway template](curriculum/orientation-takeaway.md) makes the proposed content concrete without prescribing the later product's format.

The instruction briefs are descendants of the Personal Context Portfolio in this course's design: select appropriate context, turn it into actionable guidance, test it, and revise it. This is a learning progression, not a claim about the historical origins of the file formats. Integrate the short exercise into activities 2 and 3. Explain the filenames to everyone; offer actual file placement and loading as a supported-tool extension. Learners using ordinary chat can paste the same guidance and complete the course. See [context to instructions](curriculum/context-to-instructions.md).

Use Nathaniel Whittemore's [Personal Context Portfolio](https://github.com/nlwhittemore/personal-context-portfolio) as an attributed influence. Its broader ten-file structure is a potential extension. Write a smaller original beginner activity; inspect the actual licence before copying templates.

Teach distinctions between information in the current conversation, user instructions, uploaded material, project context, product memory features, and the learner's own saved notes. These mechanisms differ by product and account. A folder name or the AI saying it remembers something is not proof of access, persistence, or isolation.

An activity asking “what do you know about me?” should require learners to check claims, correct guesses, and inspect available controls. It must not present the generated answer as a complete audit of stored data. Use fictional or low-sensitivity examples when learners prefer them.

## Proposed course shape

**Agreed launch scope:** three complete activities—one useful AI task, a personal context brief, and checking/improving a result—with accounts, saved progress, a course email, and reviewed help. This is the first usable course, expanding toward the full sequence below. The [project control file](../CONTROL.md) owns the delivery targets and prioritised wishlist.

Working estimate: ten sessions of roughly 30–35 minutes, about five to six hours total, with optional exploration toward ten hours. Ten days is a suggested rhythm, not a deadline. Duration and sequence need pilot validation.

| Session | Useful learning outcome | Example evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Find a reason to use it | Choose a personally useful goal and try one small task | Baseline attempt and a statement of what would help |
| 2. Choose how to interact | Recognise web, phone, desktop, text, voice, image, and available live modes | Try an available mode or inspect a demonstration; explain the choice |
| 3. Give it enough to work with | Improve an answer by supplying relevant context | Compare a first result with a revised result |
| 4. Make a “working with me” note | Create and edit a small personal context brief | Learner-reviewed note, with unnecessary details removed |
| 5. Find what carries forward | Inspect available memory and instruction controls | Record what was verified, what was assumed, and what remains uncertain |
| 6. Keep a project together | Give an ongoing task its own brief and instructions | Reusable project brief; use project features where available |
| 7. Work with material you already have | Use suitable text, an image, or a document | A checked result based on supplied material, with a text fallback |
| 8. Judge and improve the result | Detect a mistake or mismatch and repair it | Identify an issue, check it, and explain the revision |
| 9. Discover a next capability | Explore reminders, digests, browser assistance, or computer actions | Choose a useful next step; inspect or test it where supported |
| 10. Choose and begin a purposeful collaboration | Apply the approach to a small task and identify a worthwhile next goal | Checked result and portable context kit describing the goal, a first step, human learning needs, and information the agent needs |

Capability coverage is not a requirement to perform every modality. Live camera, browser actions, computer control, and automations need capability checks, demonstrations, and alternatives. Learners should understand the difference between an AI proposing an action and a tool actually scheduling or performing it.

Each lesson should offer a plain-language purpose, an estimated time, a short explanation or demonstration, a task in the learner's chosen context, a way to report back, a brief check, and an optional extension. External links should say what they add and how to return to the main journey.

## Experience and system requirements to carry into planning

- R1. Visitors can understand the course, preview a sample, and inspect the public design case study without enrolling.
- R2. Learners can create an account, return to saved progress, and recover access through a straightforward flow.
- R3. Initial discovery informs example choices and support without labelling learners or forcing disclosure of personal information.
- R4. Every core task has an achievable route using an accessible AI tool; paid or unavailable capabilities have alternatives.
- R5. Lessons make the next action clear and support explanation before practice. Mobile reading, switching to another app, and returning without losing work are essential test cases.
- R6. Progress captures practice and reflection. Short quizzes support retrieval and judgement; completion clicks alone are not evidence of capability.
- R7. Learners can receive useful course updates and learning reminders, control reminder frequency, pause them, and unsubscribe from optional learning emails without losing access.
- R8. A reminder returns the learner to the appropriate point in their journey. Exact pacing and catch-up behaviour remain to be decided.
- R9. Learners can ask for help with relevant lesson context. The support agent prepares a draft; Finlay reviews and approves the response before it is sent. Pending requests remain visibly pending and human edits remain possible.
- R10. Personal reflections and support records are private to the learner and authorised course staff. Public portfolio examples use synthetic material or specific permission.
- R11. Learners can understand what the course stores separately from what their chosen AI app stores. Export, deletion, retention, and context-kit storage rules must be settled before collecting pilot data.
- R12. Core learning remains usable when external links, advanced AI features, or optional AI assistance are unavailable.
- R13. Finlay can revise lessons, inspect progress and feedback, handle support, and maintain the service without editing learner records manually.
- R14. Course design and public evidence trace learner needs through outcomes, tasks, assessment, and revisions.
- R15. Maintain a compact record of project decisions, priorities, milestones, operating costs, release checks, and revisions that supports the public delivery case study.
- R16. Agent-run persona tests use clearly labelled test accounts and synthetic information. Their activity is kept separate from real enrolment, engagement, and learning reports.
- R17. Learners finish with portable context and at least one provisional personal or professional goal, including why it matters, a small next step, and an initial sign of a useful result. The goal may change as they learn.
- R18. Learners practise distinguishing something they need to learn from information or examples their agent needs. Identifying these needs is sufficient here; building a specialist knowledge collection or formal agent-evaluation suite belongs to the later programme.
- R19. Learners can complete the orientation using their own AI tool and manual transfer of prompts/context. Neither an integrated agent nor access to the later programme is required for completion.
- R20. The activities introduce self-assessment, selecting and making sense of knowledge, anticipating checks, and human-led execution through small practical prompts. These capacities run across the existing activities; framework terminology, comprehensive knowledge synthesis, and formal assessment design are not completion requirements.
- R21. Learners derive reusable personal and project instructions from their context portfolio, recognise the purpose of `CLAUDE.md` and `AGENTS.md`, and distinguish a saved document from instructions their chosen tool actually loads. Offer a file-based extension and an equivalent copy/paste route. Completion evidence includes explaining a selection and revising or retaining an instruction based on a checked result; creating a file alone is insufficient.

- R22. Public GitHub course sources and login-free AI context pages let learners deliberately share course purpose, outcomes, task guidance and versions with their chosen AI. Public publication excludes personal learner context, records and operational secrets.
- R23. Learners can inspect the shared context, check what their AI accessed, and use an equivalent paste route when AI browsing is unavailable. AI browsing, GitHub accounts and automatic instruction-file discovery are not required for completion.
- R24. Public course context has stable, versioned links and machine-readable descriptions alongside readable text. Site and repository representations derive from the same reviewed source. Guidance keeps the human in charge of choices, evaluation and submissions; a public context fetch cannot establish account progress or learning mastery.
- R25. Begin with an optional diagnostic files-and-folders activity before the AI brief. Learners identify files, folders and paths; find, copy and reopen a fictional practice file in Finder or Windows File Explorer; and distinguish local files, browser downloads and agent workspaces. Familiar learners can use the self-check and skip practice. A read-through route keeps phone and restricted-device learners included.
- R26. Integrate Finlay’s tips beside the relevant task as short, clearly labelled recommendations. Prefer inline callouts with optional detail over automatic pop-ups. Link to a practical guide, explain the benefit, and preserve a viable alternative.
- R27. Recommend Obsidian for a portable personal context portfolio, with a verified installation/vault/first-note guide. Describe it accurately as a free proprietary app using Markdown files. Neither Obsidian installation, paid sync, plugins nor giving an AI vault access is a completion requirement.
- R28. Make activities interactive where this removes unnecessary learner effort: editable structured inputs, immediately usable outputs and clear copy controls. Provide labelled fictional examples for exploration, including reasoning and prediction prompts. Preserve existing entries before replacement; distinguish example exploration from personal practice. Keep private learning notes separate from context copied to an AI and state where drafts are stored.

- R29. Carry learner-created context, success criteria and predictions into the next activity. Provide an explicit copy-to-AI and return-with-response workflow, comparison prompts and a reusable practice record. Preserve the inputs associated with an existing attempt when earlier drafts change. Offer copy/paste and app-switching guidance, optional foundations resources, and a manual route when automatic carry-over is unavailable.

- R30. After computer basics, introduce the AI interface and four input types: typed text, speech, files and pictures. Provide a dated free-provider comparison with platform/plan qualifications. Learners practise a useful instruction, inspect the result and clarify it; begin with one accessible input and explore a second where available. Distinguish dictation from live conversation and picture input from generation. Preserve a text and worked-example route, without requiring paid access or mastery of every input.


Accessibility criteria should include clear language, meaningful headings, keyboard use, readable layouts, captions or transcripts, visible feedback, and alternatives to audio or camera participation. Test with real users as well as technical checks.

## Public context for learners and their AI

Public GitHub source and login-free course-context pages are part of the design. Learners can point their chosen AI to a readable, versioned description of the course purpose, outcomes, current task and suggested learning support, then check what it actually accessed. The same material is available to inspect and paste when browsing is unavailable. Internet access inside the AI is optional; using the course site requires internet access. No GitHub account or paid AI capability is required.

Separate shared course context from learner-owned personal context and private LMS records. The AI supports explanation, practice and reflection; the human chooses, evaluates and submits their own work. Public context does not grant account access or establish saved progress. A later guided companion can extend this public interface without making autonomous LMS use an orientation requirement.

GitHub and the course site should publish matching material from one reviewed source, with fixed version references, clear ownership and update dates. Human-readable HTML and plain Markdown carry the actual guidance; structured metadata describes its relationships and available steps. Neither schema nor a conventional filename guarantees that a tool reads or follows it. Details, publication boundaries and acceptance examples are in [public AI context](curriculum/public-ai-context.md).

## Proposed evaluation

Keep evaluation useful and proportionate. Self-access delivery can still support task comparisons, optional feedback, and observed usability sessions; it does not require a large formal research study. Separate testing of the course from evidence of learning.

Compare an initial attempt and a later task using a small rubric: explain the goal, select relevant context, communicate or correct, judge the output, and choose a next action. Pair task evidence with learner-reported usefulness, time spent, confidence where relevant, and support needed. Do not equate confidence with competence.

Keep checks aligned with orientation depth: a learner can name one uncertainty about their own capability, explain why a piece of information helps, bring its useful points together briefly, and compare an attempt against a simple criterion chosen beforehand. Use these as prompts for reflection and support, not additional high-stakes tests or claims of mastery of the four pillars.

The exit activity should make progression tangible: the learner can explain a personally meaningful next goal, retain and reuse the relevant context, identify one human learning need and one piece of information the agent needs, and describe a first step and a simple way to judge its result. This is an introduction to purposeful collaboration, not certification of readiness for every specialist task. Keep tentative goals and uncertainty acceptable.

Acceptance example for R17–R19: a learner completes the third MVP activity, copies their context-and-goal document into their own notes, and uses a relevant portion in a fresh AI conversation to begin the next task. They explain what helped and what still needs clarification. The same route works when the future curriculum programme does not exist and when the learner never imports the document into another course. This is a test scenario, not a completed result.

Prototype checks should include a learner returning after several days, an unavailable AI feature, a failed external link, an email that arrives late, a support draft awaiting approval, and an experienced learner choosing an optional challenge. Define numerical pilot targets only after agreeing cohort size and baseline expectations.

### Artificial-persona walkthroughs

Finlay proposes an agent, potentially Codex, following the course in the role of a learner. Use this as a reproducible experience rehearsal and functional test. Proposed scenarios are a time-poor person who prefers an explanation first; someone using a phone and a free AI account; and an experienced user exploring project context or reminders. Add returning after a break and encountering an unavailable feature as situations, not personality stereotypes.

Give the test agent only the learner-visible instructions, a synthetic context, an intended task, and the relevant scenario constraints. It should report when the experience does not provide enough information rather than silently use its technical expertise to repair the course. Run the journey through the intended interface where possible; a text-only content review must be labelled as such. Browser and account access feasibility remains to be tested during implementation.

Capture steps taken, observable failures, questions raised, screenshots or transcripts where useful, and proposed fixes. Distinguish observed functional failures from model-generated usability judgements. Re-run affected scenarios after fixes. Use test recipients for emails and support requests, and preserve the human approval step in the support flow.

These runs can demonstrate systematic testing, issue handling, and iteration. They cannot establish human comprehension, motivation, confidence, accessibility experience, or learning gain. An agent's success may conceal problems for a non-specialist. Report synthetic findings as synthetic and pair them with a small human pilot where possible. If people have not yet participated, publish the testing evidence and state that learning outcomes have not yet been measured.

For the portfolio, success means a visitor can understand the need, follow Finlay's design and delivery decisions, try a coherent working experience, and inspect what was tested and improved. Add human learning evidence as it becomes available. Project management is demonstrated by the decisions and completed delivery, not simply by the existence of a project board.

Review the experience weekly after pilot launch: learner feedback, support, core-task freshness, test failures, costs, and a small prioritised set of improvements. Keep rough ideas in the control file's inbox so new ideas can be considered without expanding the current release automatically.

## Open scope boundaries

**Outside this implementation:** the later human–agent curriculum-design product, its specialist content-development workflows, formal agent assessments, and integration with the existing needs-analysis agent. They explain the pathway but are not hidden work packages for orientation. The present course introduces goal setting, useful context, and simple output checks at a human-orientation level.

Proposed exclusions from the first prototype: building an LMS from scratch unless existing candidates fail the essential journey; a mandatory embedded chatbot; AI grading of personal disclosures; autonomous support replies; complex integrations into learner accounts; and compulsory advanced automation. Community forums, certificates, payments, localisation, and organisational cohorts remain possible later additions rather than commitments.

The minimum public prototype and hosting machine are now selected. Resolve before implementation where consequential: lightweight completion evidence for the three activities, pilot/support capacity, platform and deployment feasibility, budget, minimum email behaviour, account/discovery friction, and storage of personal context. Naming, detailed provider guides, and screen designs can be developed alongside those decisions. The [discovery log](discovery-log.md) is the interview record.
